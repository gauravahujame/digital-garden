A digital garden is a personal website made of interlinked notes that you tend over time, rather than a feed of dated, finished posts.

## The garden and the stream

In 2015, Mike Caulfield gave a talk called *The Garden and the Stream: A Technopastoral*. He described two ways of organizing what we put on the web:

- **The stream** is chronological and conversational: Twitter, blogs, news feeds. Each item is a reaction to something happening now, and it sinks out of view as new items arrive.
- **The garden** is spatial and topical. Items link to other items. You wander through it instead of scrolling past it, and nothing is ever really "old".

Most of the web became stream. A garden pushes back on that by valuing connections over recency.

## What makes it different from a blog

| | Blog | Garden |
|---|---|---|
| Organized by | Date | Topic and links |
| State of a post | Finished at publish time | Always revisable |
| Tone | Performative, polished | Exploratory, honest about uncertainty |
| Value comes from | Individual posts | The links between notes |

> [!note] Learning in public
> Publishing half-formed ideas feels uncomfortable at first. The status tags ([[tags/seedling|seedling]], [[tags/budding|budding]], [[tags/evergreen|evergreen]]) help: they tell readers how much confidence to place in a note, so a rough note doesn't have to pretend to be finished.

## Why bother?

1. **Writing is thinking.** You don't fully know what you think until you try to explain it. See [[ideas/Evergreen notes|Evergreen notes]].
2. **Links compound.** One note is worth little. A hundred densely linked notes start to surface ideas you didn't plan, much like a [[ideas/Zettelkasten|Zettelkasten]].
3. **Low pressure.** A garden can start with a single seedling, which fits [[ideas/Gall's law|Gall's law]]: start small, keep it working, let it grow.
